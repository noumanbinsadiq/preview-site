What this is really about

The point is not the software. It is what you get to stop doing.

Right now the business runs because you and your team hold it together by hand. Every price is worked out by someone. Every follow-up is remembered by someone. Every figure that matters is assembled by someone, when it is asked for.

That works. It is also the ceiling. A business that depends on its owner checking things can only grow to the size of what its owner can check.

What a system like this changes is not speed. It is where your attention goes. When pricing, follow-up, approvals and the numbers all run themselves, the work left for you is the work only you can do — deciding what the company takes on, what it charges, who it hires, and which relationships are worth building.

Published research puts the average contractor at 35–40% of working hours on administration — forms, reports, invoices, change orders, chasing. Source. We are not going to claim a number for what you would get back — that depends entirely on how your team works. But that is the size of the thing being talked about.

The other way, if you prefer it

One piece at a time.

You have said you would rather have it done properly the first time, and the plan above is built for that. But the pieces are genuinely separate, so there is no reason you have to take them together.

Any of the twelve can be bought on its own, at the price shown against it, in whatever order suits you. Each one is delivered, paid for, and running before the next is started.

The difference between the two, honestly

  • All four stages: one decision, one agreed scope, one finish line, and the pieces are designed to fit together from the start.
  • One at a time: nothing is committed beyond the piece in front of you. It costs the same in total, but it takes longer, and two or three of the later pieces need earlier ones to exist first — the priority engine cannot rank on data nobody is collecting yet.

There is no discount for taking all four, and no penalty for not. It is a pacing decision, not a price one.

So there are no surprises

What is true, and what this will not do.

Every proposal has these. Most leave them out. They are more useful written down, so you can weigh them before deciding rather than discover them afterwards.

Not included
Running payroll — calculating, withholding or filing taxes. That is regulated and belongs with a payroll provider the system can talk to.
Not included
A "pay now" button on invoices. About 60% of your work is invoiced through your clients' own portals, where a payment link cannot reach. It would be roughly seven days of work aimed at under one invoice in ten.
Not advice
Tax figures. The system can hold a set-aside percentage you choose and show the number. It will not calculate what you owe — that is your accountant's job.
Needs time
The follow-up priority engine improves as engagement data accumulates. In its first weeks it works from job history alone and its rankings will be rougher.
Needs you
Dates assume you and your team are reachable for the things only you can decide — confirming record corrections, connecting Google and Telegram, and answering questions during a build.
Honest comparison
A general-purpose subscription is cheaper per month than building your own. It will not carry your portal and PO rules, your commission structure or your subcontractor payouts — but on price alone, renting is less.
Estimates
The $40,000–$80,000 market figures are published industry ranges, not quotes anyone has given you. The ServiceTitan and Procore figures come from third-party analyses, because neither company publishes a price. Treat all of them as the right order of magnitude.
Yours either way
Your data is yours and exportable in full, at any time, on request. The code is yours. Stopping costs nothing and switches nothing off.
